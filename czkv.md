## Q1 Can the authors provide stronger justification and ablations for the main design choices in the news regime bank and delta module? For example, what happens if the five-axis regime vector, active threshold, confidence weighting, or slow/shape/spike decomposition is removed or replaced?

This is an important point. Our motivation for these choices is that raw news is sparse, noisy, and often only weakly related to the target series, so REIN does not let text enter the forecast as a free additive feature. The active threshold is used to keep non-actionable articles from changing the forecast; confidence weighting is used because the LLM refinement can produce useful but uneven-quality event signals; the five-axis vector gives the delta module a compact description of the expected direction and type of regime change; and the slow/shape/spike split forces the residual correction to act through temporally structured channels rather than through one opaque correction vector.

We agree that the paper should make this argument empirically, not only architecturally. We will add ablations that remove the active threshold, replace confidence-weighted aggregation with simpler aggregation, reduce or remove the five-axis regime vector, and replace the slow/shape/spike decomposition with a single residual head. We will also report per-channel ablations to show what is lost when the slow, shape, or spike branch is removed. These results will be added to clarify which parts of the regime bank and delta module are actually responsible for the observed gains.

[Need additional experiments: w/o active threshold; w/o confidence weighting; reduced regime vector or embedding-only news input; single residual head; w/o slow, w/o shape, w/o spike; possibly bounded vs unbounded modulation.]

## Q2 Can the authors compare REIN with stronger text-augmented or news-aware forecasting baselines under the same news corpus? Results on more datasets, domains, or noisier corpora would also help clarify whether REIN generalizes beyond the current setting.

You are right that this comparison is missing. Our current baselines test whether REIN can improve over strong numerical forecasters and recent LLM/foundation-style time-series models, while the blanked and date-permuted regime packs test whether the improvement depends on time-aligned news content. However, those controls do not replace a direct comparison with a news-aware model that has access to the same corpus. Without that comparison, the evaluation does not fully separate "having extra news information" from the proposed bounded residual-modulation mechanism.

To address this, we will add a same-corpus text-augmented baseline. A controlled baseline will use the same retrieved news representation but feed it directly as an exogenous feature to a numerical forecaster or residual predictor, instead of using REIN's bounded slow/shape/spike modulation. If implementation constraints allow, we will also include or adapt a representative text-time-series method such as ChatTime, Time-VLM, or a comparable news-aware forecasting approach. This comparison is necessary to show that the benefit is not merely caused by giving REIN additional information.

For generalization, our current datasets already differ substantially in news quality and domain coverage: electricity demand and price use more curated domain feeds, traffic uses an open GDELT-derived stream, and gas demand uses a noisier bilingual corpus. We will make this distinction clearer in the manuscript and discuss Traffic more explicitly as a case where broader, less local news coverage limits the benefit of REIN.

## Q3 Can the authors provide evidence that the slow, shape, and spike channels correspond to meaningful temporal/event effects? A useful validation could be a synthetic experiment where news events are designed to induce known level shifts, shape changes, or spikes, so the learned residual channels can be compared against component-level ground truth.

The slow/shape/spike channels are not intended to be unconstrained latent factors. They are trained against explicitly constructed residual targets: the slow component captures the horizon-level residual shift, the shape component is constrained to be zero-mean across the horizon, and the spike component is selected by a quantile-and-rank spike mask after removing the calendar residual baseline. We also include decomposition visualizations in the appendix to show how the learned correction is distributed across these channels on real news-active examples.

That said, you are correct that these analyses do not give component-level ground truth. We will therefore add a synthetic validation in which known level shifts, intra-day shape distortions, and localized spikes are injected into real time-series windows. Because the injected perturbation is known, we can directly measure whether the corresponding channel captures the intended effect and whether energy leaks into the other channels. This will give more concrete support for the temporal interpretation of the decomposition.

[Need additional experiments: synthetic level-shift, shape-change, and spike perturbation tests. Suggested metrics: component-wise MAE/correlation, residual-energy attribution, and spike precision/recall/F1.]
